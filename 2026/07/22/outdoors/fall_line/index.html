Steep paths, water, mud, and musings

Take a topographic map of a hilly or mountainous location, maybe one you’ve been before. The one here shows a section of the Główny Szlak Beskidzki, the main trail crossing the various ranges of the Beskids in southern Poland. The trail is in red. Coincidentally, red is also the colour of my rage when I have to walk down Stus, the final peak of the ridge starting a few kilometres earlier in Kiczora.

Why? Because the path follows, almost to a T, the fall line.

What’s the fall line? In topography, it’s the fastest (and thus the steepest) line on a slope. On a topographic map, it’s easy to visualise it: just cross all the level lines at a right angle, and you’ll get it. Go back to the image, and check: right down Stus’s peak, you can see that red line happily cutting through level lines.

You can already probably see that a path going down (or up) a slope at the highest angle is not the most pleasant experience. Going up transform a hike into a martyrdom, and going down is a harsh sentence for your knees. And this in ideal conditions. Add some snow, or ice, and you end up like the man on a similar slope that dropped his water bottle in front of me while climbing. He shrugged, signalling that nothing in the world would make him repeat the climb (armed with crampons I recovered his bottle).

The true problem comes from water, however. You see, water is lazy: it only goes downhill, and it does so the fastest way possible. Which by now you know, matches the fall line. Water is amazing at erosion. Second only to humans in the short term (we’ll touch this point soon), it can carve through existing paths, often stripping the topsoil, and leaving only rocks behind.

Exactly: building your path on the fall line is all but a sure ticket to a path covered in rocks. So now you are walking this bloody path, on rather loose rocks. What happens? You slip a bit, end up on a bad footing; it doesn’t matter: a rock moves and tumbles a few metres down the path (rocks, like water, tend to only go down). Rinse (literally) and repeat: the consequence is obvious: at the bottom of your fall line path you get a wonderful accumulation of rocks, a small ravine that either messes up your ankles before the climb, or greets your already sore ankles after the descent.

Of course this version applies only if there is currently no water on the path. If, either because it’s early spring during snowmelt, or after some heavy rain, water is on the path, you are now fighting against both rocks and mud. And at the bottom of the fall line, before moving forward, water pools, adding to the existing ravine.

Fall line paths are destined to a constant, intense, erosion. So why are we even having them in first place?

Those paths are often deriving from “utility” paths, used mainly by loggers or other workers in the mountains. Usually helped by those powerful vehicles so rich in torque that a steep path doesn’t scare them, they happily move up and down the slopes.
Loggers, at time, drag felled trees behind their tractors, digging a central trench in the path which, you get it, creates a perfect addition to the fall line for water. And regardless, wheels damage the path, often dragging rocks and pushing them downhill even more efficiently than hikers.

If it was not clear, I’m not a fan of loggers. Anyway, even if the paths are not currently being used for that exploitation, they are often repurposed for recreational hiking. That’s why in the relatively low mountains of the Carpathians, where such business is efficient and widespread, fall line paths are so common. Move to higher mountains, and often you will walk to better designed paths, where the trail crosses the fall line at a shallow angle, inviting water to keep on its original trajectory.
